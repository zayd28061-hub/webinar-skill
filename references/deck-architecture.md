# The architecture — 9 acts, 120-180 slides

The running order below is from the Gadzhi / Monetise Shadow Operator webinar playbook, cross-checked against Russell Brunson's Perfect Webinar and 22 published benchmark reports. The order **is** the content. Most decks have roughly the right things in the wrong sequence, and that alone is why they fail.

```
HOOK → PAIN → GAIN → POSITION → PARADIGM SHIFT → MECHANISMS → THE CHOICE → THE OFFER → Q&A
```

Demonstrations are not an act. They are **interleaved** — 3 to 5 of them, spaced between mechanisms.

---

## Timing map (70-minute live webinar, ~150 slides)

| Act | Slides | Minutes | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 · The frame | 1-10 | 0-5 | The promise, one usable thing, who it is for |
| 1 · The pain | 11-30 | 5-15 | Their life, in their words |
| 2 · The gain | 31-40 | 15-20 | The after-state, equally specific |
| 3 · Position | 41-50 | 20-24 | Results not credentials, then the why |
| 4 · The paradigm shift | 51-72 | 24-34 | The reframe. The spine of the whole thing. |
| 5 · The mechanisms | 73-116 | 34-52 | Three steps, demonstrations between |
| 6 · The choice | 117-126 | 52-56 | Do it alone, or do it faster |
| 7 · The offer | 127-160 | 56-68 | Core, price, bonuses, guarantee, scarcity, CTA |
| 8 · Q&A | 161+ | 68+ | Answer the question underneath |

Scale the slide counts to the length. A 45-minute webinar is ~95 slides with the same proportions. Never compress the paradigm shift — cut demonstrations instead.

---

## Act 0 — The frame (slides 1-10)

**No welcome. No housekeeping. No "can everyone hear me".** The first 90 seconds decide the retention curve for the whole session.

- 1-2 · **Cover + the promise, restated.** What they will be able to DO by the end. Not what you will cover.
- 3-5 · **One usable thing, immediately.** Value before credentials. A number they can go and check this afternoon. This is why they stay past minute two.
- 6-8 · **Who this is for, and who it is not for.** Around minute five. Everyone who stays has now self-selected, and the exclusion is what makes the rest feel chosen.
- 9-10 · **The stay-to-the-end reason.** Open a loop you close in act 7. "At the end I am going to show you the thing I have never put on the internet." Name it, do not describe it.

## Act 1 — The pain (slides 11-30)

The rule is **lived specificity, in their words**. Twenty slides because you are laying down one concrete detail per slide, not because there is a lot to say.

- Wrong: "You're probably frustrated with your marketing."
- Right: "You open the dashboard at 11pm." / "Two hundred and forty leads." / "Eleven calls booked." / "You close the tab." / "You tell yourself it's the offer." / "Next month you change the offer."

Each of those is a slide. The audience is not learning anything in this act. They are deciding whether you have lived in their house. Mine the answers to Q4, the sales-call transcripts, and their DMs for exact phrasing. Use their nouns.

End the act on the cost: what this has already taken from them. One slide, quiet.

## Act 2 — The gain (slides 31-40)

Same specificity, inverted. Not "scale your business" — the specific Tuesday morning that looks different. Ten slides. Then one slide holding the gap between act 1 and act 2, because that gap is the entire sale.

## Act 3 — Position (slides 41-50)

**Results, never credentials.** Credentials show you studied it. Results show you did it with people like them.

- Wrong: "I'm a certified coach with 15 years of experience."
- Right: "I've worked with 200 entrepreneurs on the edge of divorce. 190 are still married three years later."

Numbers must be specific and verifiable. **If the user has no results yet, do not fake them.** Use the student posture instead: what you are running, what you measured, what it produced, stated as an experiment rather than an authority claim. That is credible. A student posturing as an expert is transparent and costs more than having no proof at all.

Then **the why**, 3-4 slides. Personal, real, short. The why lives here, never on slide one — on slide one it is self-indulgent, here it is earned.

## Act 4 — The paradigm shift (slides 51-72)

**The single most important mechanism in the deck.** Twenty-two slides. Build it slowly.

Most people believe their problem is tactical: find the right method, the right approach, and it will work. A paradigm shift reframes the whole understanding. **Not** "here is a better way to do what you have been trying to do." It **is** "what you have been trying to do is solving the wrong problem, here is what the actual problem is."

Its power is emotional release. The gap between how hard they have worked and what they have got finally makes sense, and it was not their fault.

Structure:
1. 51-56 · **What everyone believes.** State the conventional wisdom fairly and generously. Never strawman it.
2. 57-60 · **Where it came from and why it used to be true.** This is what makes it safe to have believed it.
3. 61-64 · **The break.** Show the specific point where it stops working, with a mechanism, not an assertion.
4. 65-69 · **The actual problem.** One sentence, then unpack it. This is your named idea. Give it a name here if it has one.
5. 70-72 · **The release.** "It was never your effort." Hold on this. Say nothing for three seconds.

## Act 5 — The mechanisms (slides 73-116)

Three steps. Never more — past three they stop believing they can do it. Roughly 14 slides each.

Each mechanism follows the same internal shape so the audience learns the rhythm:
1. **Name it** (1 slide, the name alone, big)
2. **What it is** (2-3)
3. **Why it works** — psychological or market dynamic, not just the claim (3-4)
4. **How, concretely** (4-5)
5. **Demonstration** (3-4) — vary the type each time: your own result, a client result, a detailed case study, a quick example

Teaching real, usable content here is not giving away the sale. It is the sale. They must leave able to do something. The offer is speed and support, not secret information.

**Demonstration variety rule:** if all three demonstrations are the same shape, the third one stops being believed.

## Act 6 — The choice (slides 117-126)

At 35-45 minutes in, present a genuine choice: implement it yourself, or use the product to go faster with support.

**Openly admit they could do it alone.** Six to twelve months. Rough patches. Trial and error. Some of it wasted.

This is counter-intuitive and it is the highest-leverage ten slides in the deck. It moves the audience from "I need this to succeed" — which triggers resistance — to "I could do this myself, but there might be an easier way," which is the state a person buys from. Skip this act and the offer reads as a trap.

## Act 7 — The offer (slides 127-160)

The transition from teaching to selling should be seamless. They are just following the natural flow from "here is what works" to "here is how to implement it." No tonal shift. No "so, let's talk about working together."

The sequence, each part with a psychological job:

1. **The core** (127-133) — what it is, in terms of the outcome, never the features. After 50 minutes of teaching, directness is appropriate.
2. **What it actually includes** (134-141) — the deliverable list, one per slide. People buy a list of things.
3. **The first seven days** (142-144) — Monday morning. **The single most skipped slide in every deck and the one that decides the sale.** They are not buying the six-month outcome, they cannot picture it. They are buying whether they can picture next Monday.
4. **The price** (145-146) — state it once. Do not defend it. Silence after. The instinct to justify is what makes it feel expensive.
5. **The bonuses** (147-151) — each bonus answers one specific objection from Q8. That is the only reason a bonus exists.
6. **The guarantee** (152-153) — removes the risk. If there is none, say plainly there is none and why.
7. **The scarcity** (154-155) — real, and attached to **the offer at the end of the webinar**, not to the registration page. "This is only available on this webinar. After we end, the bonuses go and the price goes to X." Never invent a fake deadline; the audience has seen a thousand.
8. **The stack** (156-157) — everything they get, restated together, creating the value gap that makes the price feel reasonable.
9. **The call to action** (158-160) — **one** action. No alternatives. No "or you can email me". Tell them exactly what happens when they click.

## Act 8 — Q&A (161+)

Prepare a slide per objection from Q8. Answer the question underneath, never the four on top — every question in the room is really the same one: *will this work for me specifically*.

Keep selling during Q&A. Most of the buying happens here, and most presenters stop.

---

## The four sequencing errors that kill decks

1. **Credentials before value.** Nobody has given you permission to be credible yet at minute two.
2. **The paradigm shift skipped or compressed to one slide.** Then the mechanisms are just tips, and tips are free.
3. **The choice omitted.** The offer then lands as pressure rather than as an option.
4. **The first week never shown.** They cannot picture Monday, so they wait, and waiting is a no.
